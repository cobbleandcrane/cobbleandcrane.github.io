Morning routine chart for toddlers: how to make one that works

By Cobble & Crane · November 27, 2026

If every morning turns into "shoes, please... shoes... SHOES," a picture routine chart can take you out of the nagging business. Toddlers and preschoolers can't read a list, but they can follow pictures, and they like being the one who knows what comes next. Here's how to set up a morning routine chart that works, how many steps to use at each age, and what to do when it stops working after a week.

Why a picture chart helps

  • It replaces your voice. Instead of reminding, you point: "What does the chart say is next?" The chart becomes the boss, not you.
  • It makes time visible. Little kids don't understand "in ten minutes," but they can see that two pictures are left.
  • It builds independence. Finishing a step and moving the marker is a small win kids can claim for themselves.

How many steps at each age

  • Age 2: 3 or 4 steps. Potty or diaper, get dressed, breakfast, brush teeth.
  • Age 3: 4 or 5 steps. Add shoes or "put pajamas in the basket."
  • Ages 4 and 5: 5 to 7 steps. Add make the bed, pack the backpack, coat and hat.

Fewer steps that actually happen beat a long chart nobody finishes. You can always add a step later.

Set it up in five steps

  1. Watch one real morning. Write down what actually happens and in what order, including where things fall apart.
  2. Pick the order once and keep it. The same order every day is the whole point. Many families put getting dressed before breakfast, since a fed toddler with no pants on tends to stay that way.
  3. Use one clear picture per step. A toothbrush, a shirt, a bowl, shoes. Cartoon pictures work better than photos of objects with busy backgrounds.
  4. Hang it at their eye level, where the routine happens: the bedroom door or the bathroom, not the fridge on the other side of the house.
  5. Give them something to move. A clothespin that slides down the chart, a magnet, a sticker or a dry-erase check. Laminate the chart (or slip it into a sheet protector) so it lasts.
Practice when nobody's in a hurryWalk through the chart together on a weekend afternoon, like a game. The first real school morning goes much better when the chart isn't brand new.

How to use it every morning

  • Point, don't repeat. When they stall, say "What's next on your chart?" and wait.
  • Let them move the marker. That's the reward for most kids. Cheer the step, not the speed.
  • Do the hard step together at first. If shoes are the battle, sit with them for shoes for a week, then step back.
  • Keep the end good. Put something they like at the bottom: a book on the couch, five minutes with a toy, or picking the music for the car.

Routine charts vs. reward charts

A routine chart shows the steps of a routine and gets used every day. A reward chart tracks one goal over time, like staying in bed all night or using the potty, with a sticker each time and something small at the end. They solve different problems. Start with a routine chart for mornings, and add a reward chart only if one specific step stays hard after a couple of weeks.

If you use a reward chart, keep it short and winnable: five or ten stickers to a small prize, not thirty. Little kids lose interest in a goal they can't see the end of.

A bedtime chart works the same way

Bath, pajamas, teeth, potty, two books, lights out. The same rules apply: few steps, same order, pictures at eye level, and something to move. Many families find the bedtime chart sticks even faster, because bedtime already has a natural order.

When it stops working

  • The novelty wore off: that's normal after a week or two. Swap the marker (a new clothespin, a new sticker sheet) or let them decorate the chart.
  • Too many steps: cut back to the four that matter most.
  • Mornings are rushed: start ten minutes earlier for two weeks while the routine settles in.
  • You're back to nagging: go back to pointing at the chart. The chart only works if it gets the last word.
  • A big change happened: new sibling, new school, daylight saving time. Expect a bumpy week, then return to the chart.

A sample morning chart for a 3-year-old

  1. Potty
  2. Get dressed
  3. Breakfast
  4. Brush teeth
  5. Shoes and coat
  6. Pick a car song
