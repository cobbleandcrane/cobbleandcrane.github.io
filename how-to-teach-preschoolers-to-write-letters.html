How to teach a preschooler to write letters: stroke order, grip and a 10-minute routine

By Cobble & Crane · November 27, 2026

Most three- and four-year-olds want to write long before their hands are ready, and most parents aren't sure where to start. You don't need a curriculum. Big lines first, then capital letters in a sensible order, a dot that shows where each letter starts, and about ten minutes at a time. Here's the order we use, a routine you can run at the kitchen table, and what's normal at each age.

Lines come before letters

Every letter is built from a few basic strokes: down, across, round and slanted. Kids usually manage them in roughly this order, though every child moves at their own pace:

  • Down and across lines: often around age 2 to 3.
  • A circle: often around age 3.
  • A cross (+): often around age 4.
  • A square, then slanted lines and an X: often around ages 4 to 5. Slants are the hardest, which is why A, K, M, V, W, X, Y and Z come last.

If a child can't copy a circle yet, letters like O, C and G will be frustrating. Spend a few weeks on wide tracing roads, loops and zigzags instead. Big, wobbly practice now saves tears later.

Wide lines beat thin onesA thick path (a road, a river, a train track) gives little hands room to wobble and still stay "on the road." Thin dotted lines ask for more control than most three-year-olds have.

Help the grip without fighting it

  • Short crayons: break crayons in half. A short piece pushes fingers into a pinch instead of a fist.
  • Chunky tools for the youngest: egg crayons or triangle crayons are easier for two- and three-year-olds to hold.
  • Work on a wall or easel: paper taped to a wall puts the wrist in a good position and builds the shoulder muscles that steady a pencil later.
  • Hand strength games: playdough, tearing paper, picking up pompoms with clothespins or tongs. These count as writing practice.

A fist grip at age three is common. Model a pinch, offer short crayons, and let it develop.

Start with capital letters

Capitals sit between the same two lines, are built from straight lines and big curves, and are harder to mix up than lowercase letters like b, d, p and q. Many preschool handwriting programs teach capitals first for these reasons. Lowercase can follow once capitals feel easy, often around age 5.

Teach letters in groups by stroke, not A to Z:

  1. Straight lines: L, F, E, H, T, I
  2. Curves: C, O, Q, G, S
  3. Lines plus curves: D, P, B, R, J, U
  4. Slants: V, A, N, M, W, X, Y, Z, K

The exception is your child's name. Most kids want to write their name first, so pull those letters forward even if they come from a later group.

Where to start matters more than neatness

Children who start letters in random places often end up with slow, tiring handwriting. Two simple rules cover almost everything:

  • Start at the top. Most letters begin at the top line and go down.
  • Down strokes before across strokes. For E: down first, then the three lines across from left to right.

A numbered dot at each starting point ("start at 1, then 2") makes the rules visible. That's how our tracing pages work, and it's how the road crew truck paints each letter in the video below: it starts on the dot and drives each stroke in order. Watching it once or twice before tracing helps.

A 10-minute routine

Pick one or two letters a session. Stop while it's still fun.

  1. Watch (1 minute): show the letter being made, in a video or with your own finger, saying the strokes out loud: "Down. Back to the top, across."
  2. Big finger tracing (2 minutes): trace it in the air with a straight arm, then on the table, then in a tray of salt or sand.
  3. Crayon tracing (3 minutes): trace a large letter on paper, starting on the numbered dot each time.
  4. On their own (2 minutes): give a starting dot and nothing else. Wobbly is fine. A correct start is the win.
  5. Use it (2 minutes): find the letter in a book, on a cereal box or on a street sign.

What's normal at each age

Age 3

Scribbles with intent, lines, maybe a circle. Some threes can make a few letters from their name. Focus on lines, grip games and big movements.

Age 4

Many fours can trace capitals and write some on their own, often their name. Letters are large, uneven and sometimes backwards. That's expected.

Age 5

Most of the capitals, with lowercase starting. Letters get smaller and sit closer to the line.

Common hiccups

  • Backwards letters: reversals are common through kindergarten and often into first grade. Point to the starting dot and try again, without making it a big deal.
  • Starting at the bottom: go back to the "start at the top" rule and the numbered dot. This is worth correcting early, gently.
  • Tired hands after two minutes: shorter sessions and more hand strength play.
  • Won't sit for it: move it to the wall, the sidewalk with chalk, or a tray of salt. Writing doesn't have to happen in a chair.
