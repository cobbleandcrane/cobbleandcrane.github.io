How to make a busy book: printing, laminating and hook-and-loop dots

By Cobble & Crane · November 27, 2026

A busy book is a binder of laminated pages with pieces that stick on with hook-and-loop dots: match the colors, build the truck, park the cars in the right spots. Toddlers love pulling pieces off and putting them back, and a good one keeps them busy at a restaurant table or during a sibling's nap. Making one takes an evening and a few supplies. Here's exactly how, plus the mistakes that make pages fall apart.

What you need

  • Printable pages: one page per activity, plus the pieces page that goes with it.
  • Cardstock: heavier paper (65 to 110 lb) holds up far better than regular printer paper. Your printer's manual lists the heaviest it can feed.
  • Laminating: a home laminator with 3 or 5 mil pouches, or self-seal laminating sheets if you don't have a machine. An office store can also laminate for you.
  • Hook-and-loop dots: self-adhesive coin dots, about 3/4 inch. One pack of a few hundred covers a whole book.
  • Scissors (or a paper trimmer) and a corner rounder if you have one.
  • Binding: a 1 to 1.5 inch binder, or a hole punch and 2 or 3 binder rings.
  • Storage: a zip pouch that fits in the binder, or a laminated envelope on each page.

Step by step

  1. Print. Color matters here (matching colors is half the fun), so print in color on cardstock. Print the pieces pages at the same scale as the boards so everything lines up.
  2. Laminate whole sheets first. Laminate the pieces page before you cut. Cutting small pieces and laminating them one by one takes forever and leaves loose edges.
  3. Cut the pieces out, leaving a thin clear border of laminate around each one so the seal holds. Round the corners. Sharp laminated corners scratch, and they're the first thing a toddler peels.
  4. Add the dots. Put the soft (loop) side on the pages and the scratchy (hook) side on the pieces. Then the pages don't snag each other when the book is closed, and loose pieces stick to the right spot.
  5. Let the glue set. Self-adhesive dots hold much better if you press them firmly and leave the book closed overnight before the first use.
  6. Bind it. Slide pages into a binder, or punch holes and use rings. Rings let you take one page to a restaurant without the whole book.
  7. Add storage. A zip pouch at the back for spare pieces, or a laminated envelope taped to each page so every page keeps its own set.
Make a spare-dot stripStick a row of soft dots on the inside cover. Loose pieces go there instead of under the couch, and you can see at a glance what's missing.

Mistakes that make pages fall apart

  • Regular printer paper: it wrinkles inside the laminate and tears at the hole punch. Use cardstock.
  • Cutting right on the paper's edge: without a clear border, the laminate peels open. Leave about 1/8 inch.
  • Dots on dusty or curved surfaces: wipe the laminate first and press each dot for a few seconds.
  • Too many pieces per page: for a two-year-old, four to six pieces is plenty. Add pages, not pieces.

Safety first

Busy book pieces are small. A common rule of thumb: anything that fits through a toilet paper tube is a choking risk for young children. Keep pieces large for the youngest, check for peeling dots and torn laminate every so often, and supervise kids under three.

What goes inside: page ideas by age

Ages 2 to 3

  • Color matching: put each colored truck in the garage of the same color.
  • Shape matching: circle, square and triangle cut-outs onto their outlines.
  • Traffic light: red, yellow and green circles in the right order.
  • Big and small: sort pieces onto two parking lots.

Ages 3 to 4

  • Build a truck from shapes: a rectangle body, square cab and circle wheels.
  • Count and match: dots on a dump truck matched to number cards 1 to 5.
  • Patterns: finish a row of cones (orange, white, orange, white).
  • Letter matching: capital letters onto their spots, starting with the letters in their name.

Ages 4 to 5

  • Ten frames: fill the frame with the right number of cones.
  • A number road: put numbers 1 to 10 in order along a road.
  • Capital to lowercase matching.
  • Picture sequencing: what happens first, next and last.

How to use it so it lasts

  • Save it for special times: a busy book that only comes out at restaurants, waiting rooms or quiet time stays interesting for months.
  • Show one page at a time: sit with them for the first try on a new page, then let them repeat it alone.
  • Rotate pages: keep a few pages out of the binder and swap them in every couple of weeks.

Time and cost

A 15 to 20 page book takes about two hours: half an hour to print and laminate, an hour of cutting and dots, and the rest to bind and sort. If you already have a printer, expect to spend roughly $20 to $40 on cardstock, pouches, dots and a binder, plus a laminator if you buy one. All of it is reusable for the next book.
